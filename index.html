Will AI replace lawyers?

Author: Clayton Choy

A month of legal work, done in one hour.That is what an AI tool now does at one national law firm. If a machine can do in an hour what used to take a lawyer a month, what happens to the lawyers, and to the students who hope to become them? To find out, I interviewed six practicing attorneys.

I interviewed six attorneys whose work covers litigation, patent law, construction disputes, commercial law, government investigations and business transactions. Each one answered the same ten questions about how AI is changing legal work today and how it may change the profession over the next ten years.

All six already use AI in their practice. Their answers fall into four parts:

Part II · Disagreement

Where their predictions split

When the questions turned to the next ten years, the attorneys' answers differed on four issues: how many lawyers will be needed, which areas of law will change most, what AI means for people without lawyers, and whether the legal system will be better off.

Disagreement 1

Will there be fewer lawyers?

Yes, significantly fewer

If AI does the work of junior lawyers, firms will hire fewer of them. One litigator predicted that a legal team of five to seven lawyers on a major case could shrink to three or four.

"We will not need as many lawyers coming out of law school."

Partner, trial & appellate group, Jackson Walker20 years; government investigations

No, demand is growing

Others argue that AI is creating new legal work. Clients need help understanding the laws and regulations around AI itself, and retiring lawyers must still be replaced.

"In 2026, AI has only increased the demand for legal services."

Commercial attorney, Duane MorrisBusiness-to-business disputes in the energy and health care industries

Disagreement 2

Which areas of law will change the most?

The attorneys named different areas, but a pattern appeared: the more routine and lower-stakes the work, the more exposed it is. Several litigators, however, argued that both contract work and litigation require too much judgment for any whole field to be replaced.

Most affected

  • Routine, low-stakes matters such as traffic tickets and minor misdemeanors
  • Real estate documents, wills and trusts
  • Standard contracts, employment agreements and waivers
  • First-pass document review
  • Much of family law

Least affected

  • Arguing a client's case in court
  • High-stakes matters that require decades of experience
  • Criminal law, especially prosecution
  • Strategy, negotiation and complex decisions
  • Judging, especially appeals
Disagreement 3

Is AI helping people who represent themselves in court?

Three attorneys raised an effect I did not expect. People who cannot afford a lawyer, called pro se litigants, now use chatbots to write their own court filings. The attorneys disagreed on whether this is progress or a problem.

Court records show how quickly this has grown. A public database maintained by researcher Damien Charlotin at HEC Paris tracks every court decision it can find in which a filing contained false, AI-generated material, such as invented cases or fake quotations. The first well-known case was in New York in June 2023. By September 2026, the database listed 2,046 cases. About two-thirds of them (1,397) were in U.S. courts, and the rest came from more than 30 other countries, led by Canada, Australia and the United Kingdom. Most were filed by people without lawyers.

Five U.S. cases where lawyers were caught filing fake, AI-invented citations

In each case, a lawyer filed court papers that relied on AI and included legal sources that did not exist. The judge discovered the errors and punished the lawyers responsible.

  1. June 2023Mata v. AviancaFederal court, New York City
    The lawsuit
    A passenger sued the airline Avianca, saying a metal serving cart injured his knee during a flight.
    What went wrong
    His lawyers used ChatGPT for research. Their brief cited six court cases that ChatGPT had invented, complete with fake quotations.
    Penalty$5,000 fine for the two lawyers and their firm. This was the first widely reported case of its kind.
  2. February 2025Wadsworth v. WalmartFederal court, Wyoming
    The lawsuit
    A family sued Walmart, saying a hoverboard bought there caught fire and burned their home.
    What went wrong
    A lawyer used his law firm's own AI tool to draft a motion. Eight of the nine cases it cited did not exist.
    Penalty$5,000 in combined fines for three lawyers. The lawyer who drafted it was removed from the case.
  3. July 2025Coomer v. LindellFederal court, Colorado
    The lawsuit
    A former voting-machine company employee sued MyPillow founder Mike Lindell for defamation.
    What went wrong
    Lindell's lawyers filed a brief with nearly 30 defective citations, including cases that did not exist. One lawyer admitted using AI.
    Penalty$3,000 fine for each of the two lawyers.
  4. December 2025Couvrette v. WisnovskyFederal court, Oregon
    The lawsuit
    A family dispute over the ownership of a winery, which also included claims of elder abuse.
    What went wrong
    The plaintiffs' lawyers filed three briefs containing 15 cases that did not exist and 8 made-up quotations.
    PenaltyAbout $110,000 in fines and legal fees, the largest AI penalty reported so far. The judge also threw out the plaintiffs' entire case.
  5. 2026Withers v. City of AberdeenFederal court, Northern Mississippi
    The lawsuit
    An attorney sued the city of Aberdeen, Mississippi, over unpaid bills for his legal work.
    What went wrong
    Lawyers on both sides used AI to draft filings and did not check the cases it cited, several of which were fake.
    PenaltyAll four lawyers were removed from the case and fined. Two were banned from that federal court for two years, and the trial was canceled.

Sources: court decisions as reported by LawSites, Mondaq, FindLaw, Bloomberg Law and the HAQQ and GC AI sanctions trackers (2025–2026).

A third attorney noted that responding to these filings adds work for lawyers on the other side. Still, the partner concerned about the courts believes AI will improve the system overall, because it can make legal help affordable for people who could never pay for a lawyer.

Part III · Law School

What this means for future lawyers

The work AI does best is the work that new lawyers have always started with. That affects how many graduates firms hire, how new lawyers are trained, and how students should prepare.

Hiring

Law firms are hiring fewer new graduates

Hiring data shows a gradual decline. Firms are offering fewer summer positions, the largest firms have not grown their first-year classes since 2023, and fewer graduates found law firm jobs in 2025 than in 2024.

AI is one likely cause among several. Clients have pushed back on legal costs, firms increasingly hire experienced lawyers from other firms, and companies are keeping more legal work in-house. However, industry analysts note that firms were using AI widely by 2025, so it is reasonable to conclude that AI now plays a role.

Training

How will new lawyers learn?

When asked what concerned them most, the attorneys mentioned false information and confidentiality. The concern they returned to most often, however, was that new lawyers might stop thinking for themselves.

One partner has seen associates submit AI-generated work without understanding it. Another warned that young lawyers face a strong temptation to accept AI's answers without question. A third explained that routine tasks are how lawyers build and maintain their skills. If AI performs those tasks, firms will need a new way to train beginners.

Advice

The attorneys' advice for students considering law school

None of the attorneys advised against going to law school. All of them, however, said the path into the profession is changing.

Aim for a top-20 law school

With firms hiring fewer new lawyers, she advised attending one of the 20 highest-ranked law schools in the country, often called a "T20" school. Graduates of lower-ranked schools may struggle to find jobs. If law is truly your passion, pursue it, but build a strong résumé and be prepared to compete.

Partner, trial & appellate group, Jackson Walker20 years; government investigations

Part IV · Conclusions

What it all adds up to

Taken together, the interviews and the data point to four conclusions.

1

The change is real, but it is gradual

By 2026, 81% of legal professionals used AI at least weekly, and known court cases involving AI errors grew from 87 in May 2025 to more than 2,000 in September 2026. Hiring, however, is declining slowly rather than collapsing. The attorneys agree on what AI can do today. They disagree about how far and how fast it will go.

2

Entry-level work is changing first

Document review, first drafts and routine matters are where AI performs best, and those tasks have traditionally belonged to new lawyers. Both the interviews and the hiring numbers show the effects starting at the bottom of the profession.

3

The profession faces a training problem

The skills the attorneys believe AI cannot replace, such as judgment, strategy and advocacy, were developed through years of the same routine work that AI now performs. Law firms and law schools will need new ways to train the next generation.

4

The largest effect may be outside law firms

People without lawyers are already using AI to go to court on their own. This could make the legal system more accessible, but it also places new strain on courts. How courts respond may matter as much as how law firms adapt.

So, will AI replace lawyers?

Not according to any of the attorneys I interviewed. But it will change what lawyers do.

AI is taking over much of the work that once filled a lawyer's day, and the profession will likely need fewer people, especially at the entry level. The lawyers who remain will be valued for what AI cannot provide: sound judgment, persuasive advocacy, empathy for clients and the ability to recognize when the AI is wrong.

For students considering law school, the message is not to stay away. It is to prepare differently: aim for a top-ranked school, learn to use AI well, and never let it replace your own thinking.
